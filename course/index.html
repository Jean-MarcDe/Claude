A two-part course for people who use AI at work

Tokens to Decisions

Part One explains how a modern language model actually works, from gradient descent to agents. Part Two spends that understanding on the decisions you make every time you use one: which model, what it costs, what to put in front of it, how much to supervise, how to break up a large task, and how to know whether the result is right.

Two parts · 14 modules · 20 diagrams · 20 exercises · a 5-stage workshop
Reading time ≈ 5.5 hours · Full workshop ≈ 2 days
Audience: anyone who uses AI tools for professional work — engineers, analysts, researchers, project managers.
No programming experience required. No maths beyond school algebra.

Why the theory comes first

Most people learn AI tools as a list of tricks: phrase it this way, add that instruction, pick the newer model. The tricks work until they don't, and then nobody can say why — because the tricks were never connected to a mechanism.

Almost every practical rule in Part Two is a direct consequence of something mechanical in Part One. Attaching every document is a bad idea because the context is zero-sum and attention thins as it fills. A model cannot be trusted to total a spreadsheet column because next-token prediction is not arithmetic. You cannot instruct your way out of a hidden message in a supplier's document, because the model reads instructions and data through the same channel. None of that is memorisable as a tip. All of it is obvious once you can see the machine.

So Part One builds the machine, developing three worked examples that recur as the concepts accumulate — a neuron learning logical AND, one sentence carried from tokenisation through to sampling, and a payment function that an assistant is asked to modify. Part Two then uses the same three, and names the mechanism behind each recommendation it makes.

The bridge

This table is the spine of the course. Read it now as a promise, and again at the end as a summary.

Mechanism (Part One)Consequence you can act on (Part Two)
The context window is a fixed token budgetAttaching every document crowds out the passages that matter. Curate a brief.
Attention thins as the context fills, and is weakest in the middleA supported window and a usable window are different claims. Extract before you attach.
Tokenisation is tuned for EnglishThe same instruction costs 1.5–2× more in German or Japanese. Write re-sent text in English.
A stable context prefix can be cachedKeep instructions stable; a preamble that changes each run can cost several times more.
The model is stateless; the software re-sends everything“It forgot” is a context-management problem. Write handover notes deliberately.
Output is sampled from a probability distributionOne run is one draw. Never compare two models, or two prompts, on a single attempt.
Next-token prediction is a poor substrate for arithmeticRoute calculations to a tool, and check one value by hand.
Hallucination is the normal behaviour of a likelihood machineGround every factual claim in supplied material, and require a citation for each.
Instructions and data share one input channelA supplier's document is executable input. Limit what the assistant can reach.
A forward pass has fixed computational depthPlan as a separate, cheap step rather than demanding depth in a single answer.
Each context is independentDelegation loses everything the specification leaves out. Write the handover.
Fluent output carries no signal about correctnessWrite acceptance criteria before the work, not after.

How to run it

The material is one thing; the format is yours. Four that work:

Skim

2.5 hours · solo

Read the concept map, Modules 1 to 4 of Part One, then Modules 8, 9 and 14 of Part Two. Enough to stop making the expensive mistakes.

Workshop

2 days · 6–20 people

Day one: Part One with the inline exercises worked together. Day two: Part Two, ending with the five-stage workshop run on a task the group actually owns.

Study group

7 weeks · 1 hour weekly

One module per week, one volunteer presenting, the exercises done in advance. Slowest, and it sticks best — the discussion is where the real objections surface.

Reference

ongoing

When someone asks why attaching all three reports made the answer worse, send the Module 9.2 anchor rather than the answer.

What you need

For Part One, nothing at all. No programming, no maths beyond school algebra, and every figure is self-contained. The three worked examples — a neuron learning logical AND, a single sentence carried through tokenisation to sampling, and the supplier assessment — are developed from scratch where they appear.

For Part Two, access to an AI assistant you can use on real work, and a task of your own to bring to the workshop in Module 14. The practical environment described is GitHub Copilot in VS Code, because that is what the organisation provides, but the concepts transfer to other tools and the text says which parts are specific. Some sections describe an assistant writing and running a short script on your behalf; you are never asked to write one, and the course explains what such a script does and how to check it.

Before you start

Part Two names specific products, prices and features. Those move faster than any document can, and several of them changed while this course was being written. Volatile facts appear in dated tables saying what was checked and against what; treat them as pointers rather than as a current price list, and confirm anything you are about to standardise on. The core mechanisms in Part One — gradient descent, attention, sampling — have been stable for years, though several topics in its later modules remain areas of active research.
