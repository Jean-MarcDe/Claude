Part Two

Operating AI systems

Seven modules that spend Part One. Choosing a model and knowing what it costs, deciding what to put in front of it, picking how much autonomy to grant, breaking a large task into pieces, making your preferences stick, packaging what works into something reusable, and establishing whether the result can be trusted.

Modules 8–14 · ≈2 hours reading · 5 diagrams · 9 exercises · a 5-stage workshop

The supplier assessment from Module 5 runs through all of it. Three vendor reports of forty to sixty pages, a spreadsheet of about twelve thousand measurements, and a memo due before a decision meeting. Each module takes the same task further: Module 8 decides which model should do it and what that will cost, Module 9 decides what the model gets to see, Module 10 decides how much to supervise, Module 11 breaks it into parts, Modules 12 and 13 make the result repeatable, and Module 14 establishes whether it is right.

Two kinds of statement in this part

Mechanisms — how context budgets work, why delegation costs something, what a tool call is — are as durable as anything in Part One. Products, prices, credit allowances and feature names are not: they change monthly, and several of them changed while this course was being written. Volatile facts appear in dated tables rather than in prose, and each says what it was checked against. Where a figure depends on your organisation's own configuration, the table names what an administrator has to confirm, because that is actionable and a vague warning is not.

No programming required

Nothing in this part asks you to write code. Some of it describes an assistant writing and running a short script on your behalf — to total a column, or to reshape a table — and where that happens the course explains what the script does and how to check that it did it, which is the part that matters. The practical environment is GitHub Copilot in VS Code, because that is what the organisation provides, but the concepts transfer and the text says which parts are specific to it.

Module 8

Choosing a model, and what it costs

The first decision in any piece of AI-assisted work, and the one most often made by accident. This module covers what actually differs between models, how to read a benchmark without being misled by it, and how the money works — tokens, credits and the considerably larger cost that neither of them measures.

8.1

What actually differs between models

Providers publish families of models that share training data and architecture but differ in size and configuration. Within a family, the larger model is slower and more expensive and performs better on difficult tasks; the smaller one is faster and cheaper and performs comparably on easy ones. That much is uncontroversial. The useful question is what “difficult” means in practice, and the answer is more specific than general intelligence.

Several distinct properties vary, and they do not vary together. A model can be excellent at one and mediocre at another, which is why a single ranking is a poor guide.

PropertyWhat it meansWhere it shows up in the supplier assessment
Depth on hard problemsMulti-step inference where an early mistake propagatesReconciling three incompatible definitions of “throughput”
Instruction followingHonouring constraints precisely, including negative ones“Never present a vendor claim as a measurement” actually holding across four pages
Structured outputProducing a required shape reliably, every timeA comparison table with the same columns for all three vendors
Tool useChoosing the right tool, passing correct arguments, interpreting resultsCalling a spreadsheet function rather than estimating a median
Long-context handlingUsing material far from the end of the windowRecalling a caveat from page 6 of report A while reading report C
LatencyTime to first token and overall completion timeWhether a conversational back-and-forth stays usable
CostPrice per token in and outWhether running the task weekly is sensible

Two of these deserve expanding because they are commonly misunderstood.

Reasoning effort is now a configurable quantity rather than a fixed property of a model. Module 5.5 established the mechanism: generating intermediate steps buys serial computation, because a single forward pass has fixed depth. Many current models expose this as a setting — minimal, low, medium, high, or a token budget — and the setting has a larger effect on both quality and cost than the choice between two adjacent models in the same family. A small model told to think hard will outperform a large model told to answer immediately on a genuinely multi-step problem, and will cost less doing it. Treat effort as a dial you set per task rather than a property you inherit.

Long-context capability is two claims that vendors report as one. The advertised window is how many tokens the system will accept. How well the model uses material in the middle of that window is a separate question, measured by separate evaluations, and the two diverge considerably. Module 4.7 explained why. A model advertising a million-token window may be markedly less reliable at 400,000 tokens than a model advertising 200,000 is at 150,000, and the specification sheet will not tell you that.

8.2

Reading benchmarks honestly

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks with known answers, run against a model under stated conditions, reported as a score. Public leaderboards aggregate many such scores into a ranking. They are genuinely useful as a first filter and genuinely misleading as a final answer, and knowing which failure modes apply lets you use them for the first without falling into the second.

An aggregate index hides its weighting. Composite scores combine categories — reasoning, coding, agentic tool use, general knowledge — with weights chosen by whoever built the index. Those weights encode a view about what matters. An index weighting agentic tasks heavily will rank models differently from one weighting scientific reasoning heavily, and neither ranking is wrong; they are answering different questions. If your work resembles one category, look at that category's score rather than the headline.

Conditions vary and are often not comparable. The same model produces different scores at different reasoning-effort settings, with different system prompts, with different sampling parameters, and with or without tool access. A leaderboard comparing two models may be comparing two configurations as much as two models.

Contamination is a structural problem. Benchmarks are published, which means their questions and answers eventually appear in text that later models are trained on. A model that has seen the answers scores well without having the capability the benchmark was meant to measure. The field's response is to retire saturated benchmarks and introduce harder ones, which helps but means scores are not comparable across time.

Saturation compresses differences. When several models score above ninety per cent, the remaining gap is mostly noise and the residual questions are often the ambiguous ones. Reported confidence intervals are worth checking: a one-point difference with a one-point interval is not a difference.

The benchmark is not your task. This is the one that matters most. A model that excels at competition mathematics may be unremarkable at reconciling inconsistent terminology across three badly written vendor reports, because nothing in the benchmark resembles that.

Used properly, a public leaderboard narrows a field of thirty models to a shortlist of three or four worth testing. That is a real service. It cannot tell you which of those three is better at your work, and no amount of reading will make it tell you, because it does not contain that information.

8.3

Building a benchmark of your own

The remedy is a small internal evaluation, and it is far less work than it sounds. The aim is not scientific rigour but a defensible answer to one question: for this kind of task, which of these candidates should we use?

Collect between five and ten tasks you have genuinely done, where you already know what a good answer looks like. For the supplier assessment, that might be last quarter's comparison, where you know which vendor's claim turned out to be unsupported. Write down, for each, what a correct result must contain — the specific figures, the conflicts that should be caught, the assumptions that should be listed. These are your acceptance criteria, and writing them is most of the work.

Then run each task against each candidate model, three times, because output is sampled and one run tells you about one draw rather than about the model. Score against the criteria rather than by impression. Record cost and elapsed time alongside the score, since those are part of the comparison.

Three runs across eight tasks and three models is seventy-two runs, which sounds like a great deal and is typically an afternoon, most of it waiting. The output is worth considerably more than a leaderboard position: it tells you where the models actually differ on work you actually do, and it becomes a regression suite you re-run when a provider updates something.

Exercise 8.1

A colleague reports that Model A “is clearly better” than Model B, having tried each once on the same question. Using Module 4.5, give two reasons this evidence is weak, and state the smallest experiment that would settle it.

Expected answer

Output is sampled from a distribution, so a single run from each is one draw from each — the difference observed may be entirely within the variation either model shows against itself. And a single task is a single point on a jagged frontier, so even a real difference on that question may reverse on the next one. The smallest useful experiment is several tasks representative of the real work, each run three or more times per model, scored against criteria written before the runs rather than after. Writing the criteria first matters: criteria written afterwards tend to describe whichever output the author preferred.

8.4

Tokens as the unit of cost

Module 2.1 introduced tokens as the unit a model reads. They are also the unit you are billed in, and the connection between the two is exact: every token that enters the context is processed, and processing is what costs money.

Providers price in several categories, and the distinctions matter more than their names suggest.

CategoryWhat it coversTypical relative price
Input tokensEverything in the context: instructions, documents, history, tool resultsBaseline
Cached input tokensA context prefix the provider has already processed and can reuseOften around a tenth of input
Output tokensEverything the model generatesCommonly several times input
Reasoning tokensInternal deliberation, often not shown to youBilled as output

Three of these produce surprises on a first bill.

Output is the expensive side. Generation is sequential — one forward pass per token, as Module 4.6 established — whereas input can be processed in parallel. The price difference reflects that. A request that reads a long document and returns three sentences is cheap; one that reads a short brief and returns twenty pages is not.

Caching rewards stable prefixes. When the beginning of your context is identical to the previous request, the provider can reuse the computation. This is why keeping instruction files stable matters financially, and why a workflow that rewrites its preamble each time pays considerably more for identical work. The saving is large: the worked example in 8.6 shows an agentic workflow costing a quarter of what it would without caching.

Reasoning tokens are invisible and billed. A model set to high effort may generate thousands of tokens of internal deliberation that never appear in the answer, and they are charged at the output rate. This is the single largest source of unexpected cost, and it is also the thing you are paying for when extended reasoning is worth it — the point is not to avoid it but to know it is happening.

Two further effects come from the loop rather than from a single call. In an agentic workflow the context grows at every step as tool results accumulate, so step fourteen processes everything from steps one to thirteen. And a single instruction from you may cause many model calls: Module 5.9's loop runs until it stops, and nothing about your one sentence constrains how many iterations that takes.

8.5

Credits, requests and quotas

Tokens are a technical unit. The units you are actually billed in are commercial, defined by whoever sells you access, and the two are related by a conversion that the provider chooses and can change. Keeping them separate prevents most confusion about AI bills.

UnitWhat it isWho defines it
TokenA chunk of text the model processesThe model's tokeniser — a technical fact
Model callOne request to one model, with its own contextThe system architecture
Your instructionOne thing you asked forYou — and it may cause many model calls
CreditAn accounting unit with a stated monetary valueThe vendor, by policy
Allowance / quotaCredits included with a seat per periodThe vendor and your plan
OverageUsage beyond the allowance, billed or blockedYour administrator's configuration

The row that causes the most trouble is the third. It is natural to assume that one thing you asked for equals one request equals one unit of billing, and in an agentic workflow that assumption fails by an order of magnitude. Asking an assistant to compare three vendor reports may produce a planning call, a dozen tool calls each followed by a model call to interpret the result, several subagent invocations with their own contexts, and a final synthesis — perhaps twenty model calls from one sentence. This is not waste; it is the loop doing what Module 5.9 describes. But it means a per-seat allowance expressed in credits cannot be divided by the number of questions you expect to ask.

Ask these before running anything at volume.

8.6

Four ways to do one task

The supplier assessment, costed four ways. The rates below are illustrative, chosen round to keep the arithmetic legible; substitute your own current figures and the method is unchanged.

Illustrative rates, US$ per million tokens input cached output large model 3.00 0.30 15.00 small model 0.25 0.025 1.25 Material: three reports ≈ 100 000 tokens · measurements summarised ≈ 12 000 tokens
ApproachWhat happensCostCredits
1 · One large modelEverything into one context, one pass, 3 000-token memo$0.38438
2 · One small modelIdentical inputs on the cheap model$0.0323
3 · Large plans, small executesLarge model plans and synthesises; three extractions and the data summary on the small model$0.13514
4 · Agentic loopLarge model, 14 steps, tool call each step, context grows to ~51 000 tokens$0.36537

Several things in that table are worth more than the numbers themselves.

Option 2 is twelve times cheaper than option 1 and is not therefore twelve times better value. The question the table cannot answer is whether the small model reconciles three incompatible definitions of throughput correctly, and if it does not, the cheap memo is worth nothing. Cost comparisons are only meaningful between options that both meet your acceptance criteria, which is why Module 8.3 comes before this one.

Option 3 costs about a third of option 1 by putting the expensive model only where judgement is needed. The extraction work — find every throughput figure in this report and report it with its page number — is mechanical and a small model does it well. The reconciliation is not mechanical. Module 11 develops this pattern properly, including the overheads it introduces, which the arithmetic here ignores.

Option 4 is the one that surprises people. Fourteen steps sounds expensive and the total lands near option 1 — because the context is largely identical between steps and therefore cacheable. Of roughly 410 000 input tokens processed across the run, about 375 000 are cached reads. Without caching the same workflow costs $1.378, nearly four times as much. This is the strongest practical argument for stable prefixes, and it explains why a workflow that shuffles its instructions between steps can cost several times one that does not.

One anchor for intuition: at an allowance in the region of 3 900 credits per seat per month, a 38-credit task is around one per cent of a monthly allowance. Whether that is comfortable depends entirely on how often it runs, and that is the calculation to do before automating anything.

Exercise 8.2

You run option 4 weekly. Someone proposes adding a step at the start that reformats the instruction block differently on each run, to “keep the model's attention fresh”. Using the figures above, estimate what that proposal costs per year.

Expected answer

Changing the prefix on every run invalidates the cache, so each run moves from $0.365 towards the uncached $1.378 — about $1.01 more per run, or roughly $53 a year for one person running it weekly. Trivial alone; multiplied across a department running several such workflows daily it becomes real money for a change with no mechanism behind it. Attention does not work the way the proposal assumes, and Module 4.7 is where to look for why.

8.7

The cost that actually matters

Every figure so far has been the model's cost, and for most knowledge work it is not the dominant term.

Suppose the memo takes twenty minutes to verify properly — opening the cited pages, checking two or three figures against the spreadsheet, confirming that the conflicts named are real. At a loaded cost of around US$60 an hour, that verification costs about $20. The model cost was $0.38. The human time is roughly fifty times the model time, and this ratio is typical rather than extreme.

The conclusion reorders the whole problem. Optimising token spend is optimising the small term. What actually moves the total cost of getting a correct memo is the number of times you have to send it back, how long verification takes, and whether a wrong figure survives into a decision. A model that costs three times as much and removes one round of rework is overwhelmingly the cheaper choice.

So the components to weigh are the model cost, the elapsed time before you can act, the number of retries before the output is acceptable, the human time to verify it, and the cost of a mistake that gets through. The last of these is the one that should dominate a procurement recommendation and does not appear on any invoice.

This produces a usable framing. For any task, three quantities are in tension: quality, cost and latency. Improving one usually costs you another, and the set of options where you cannot improve any one without sacrificing another is the frontier of sensible choices. Options inside that frontier are strictly worse than something else available and should simply be discarded. The real decision is where on the frontier a given task belongs — and that depends on the consequence of being wrong, which is a judgement about the work rather than a property of any model.

Module 9

Context engineering and prompting

Part One established that the parameters are fixed and the context is the only input you control. This module is that sentence turned into practice: deciding what the model sees, what it must not see, and what happens to all of it over a long session.

9.1

What is in the context

When you type a question and press return, the text you typed is a small fraction of what the model receives. Understanding the rest is the difference between operating these systems and guessing at them.

A typical request carries the vendor's own system prompt, which establishes identity, formatting conventions and safety behaviour and may run to thousands of tokens you cannot see or change. It carries the description and parameter schema of every tool currently available. It carries whatever persistent instructions you or your organisation have configured, which Module 12 covers. It carries material gathered automatically from your working environment — the documents you have open, the files in the folder you are working in. It carries passages retrieved by a search over whatever corpus the system has indexed. It carries the conversation so far, including every tool result returned during it. And then it carries your sentence.

Two consequences follow immediately. The same words produce different results in different tools and in different folders, because the surrounding context differs — which explains most reports of a model behaving inconsistently. And the components you influence are the instructions, the material you supply and the conversation you allow to accumulate, which is why those three occupy the rest of this module.

9.2

Supported context and usable context

A vendor's stated context length is a capacity claim: this many tokens will be accepted without error. It is not a claim about how reliably material anywhere in that span will be used, and Module 4.7 explained the mechanism behind the gap. Attention distributes a fixed total weight across all positions, so the more positions there are, the less weight any one receives; and material near the beginning and end is attended to more reliably than material in the middle.

The practical consequence is that filling a large window is not a way of ensuring the model has everything it needs. Beyond some point it is a way of ensuring that what it needs is somewhere in the middle. For the supplier assessment this is immediately concrete: loading all three reports in full gives the model about 100 000 tokens of material in which the four paragraphs that actually conflict are buried, whereas extracting the relevant sections first gives it 6 000 tokens in which they are prominent. The second produces better answers and costs a fifteenth as much.

Models differ in how gracefully they degrade, and the only way to know how yours behaves is to test it on your material. A usable check: place a specific, checkable fact at the start, the middle and the end of a realistic context, and ask a question that requires it. If the middle placement fails and the others succeed, you have found the boundary of usable context for that model on that kind of material, and it is probably well short of the advertised figure.

9.3

Choosing what to include

Context selection is a budgeting problem with a quality term attached. Every token spent on something irrelevant costs money, occupies space that relevant material could use, and dilutes the attention available to everything else.

The disposition that works is to treat the context as a brief prepared for a capable colleague who has no background on the matter and will read exactly what you give them and nothing else. Such a brief contains the specific sections that bear on the question, not the complete documents. It states what the deliverable is. It names the constraints. It includes one example of the output format if the format matters. And it leaves out everything that does not serve those.

Applied to the supplier assessment, a first attempt usually attaches all three PDFs and the whole spreadsheet, which is both expensive and ineffective. A better brief contains the performance sections of each report with page references preserved, a summary table computed from the measurements rather than the twelve thousand raw rows, the four evaluation criteria the decision will actually turn on, and a note saying which vendor's terminology differs from the others. That is perhaps eight thousand tokens doing more work than a hundred thousand.

Two refinements repay the effort. Say explicitly what to ignore, since an instruction to disregard the marketing sections is as useful as one pointing at the technical appendix. And put the decisive constraint last, where attention is strongest — if the memo must not present vendor claims as measurements, that sentence belongs at the end of the brief rather than buried in the middle.

Exercise 9.1

You have 100 000 tokens of vendor reports and a 200 000-token window, so everything fits. Give two distinct mechanical reasons for extracting the relevant sections anyway, and one circumstance in which loading everything is the better choice.

Expected answer

First, cost: you pay for every input token on every request, and in a conversation the whole context is re-sent each turn, so a hundred-thousand-token context is charged repeatedly. Second, attention: the relevant passages are competing with ninety-four thousand tokens of irrelevant material, and the lost-in-the-middle effect applies to precisely the content you care about. Loading everything is better when you do not yet know what is relevant — an exploratory first pass whose purpose is to find out which sections matter, after which you switch to a curated context for the actual work.

9.4

Long sessions, growth and compaction

In a conversation, and far more sharply in an agentic workflow, the context grows monotonically. Each turn appends your message and the reply; each tool call appends its result. Nothing is removed, because nothing in the mechanism removes it.

Eventually the window fills, and the harness must do something. Which something it does is a design decision made by the tool, and the options have different failure modes. Dropping the oldest turns is simple and silently discards your original instructions, which were usually the most important thing present. Summarising early history into a short note preserves the gist and loses specifics, so a precise figure mentioned in turn three becomes an approximate recollection. Retrieving only the relevant parts of the transcript works well when retrieval works and fails invisibly when it does not. Some systems do this automatically and call it compaction; some ask; some simply refuse further input.

Knowing which your tool does explains a great deal of its behaviour at the edges, and it is worth finding out deliberately rather than discovering it during important work.

The practical habits follow from the mechanism rather than from preference. Work on one task per session, because a session that has drifted through three unrelated problems is carrying three problems' worth of material that is now diluting the fourth. Restart rather than argue: after three failed corrections the context contains three wrong approaches, and the model is being consistent with them exactly as Module 4.6 describes, so a fresh session opening with what you learned is more likely to succeed than a fourth correction. Restate the decisive constraint after any long detour, since it has drifted towards the middle. And when a long session starts producing worse results, recognise that nothing degraded — the context filled with material that is no longer relevant, and the remedy is a new session rather than a better prompt.

One deliberate technique is worth naming because it solves a real problem. When a long piece of work must continue across sessions, end each session by having the assistant write a short handover note — what was decided, what remains, which figures are confirmed — and begin the next session with that note rather than with the old transcript. You are choosing what survives rather than letting a truncation algorithm choose for you, and it is the single most effective habit for work that spans days.

9.5

Writing a task specification

Prompting is often taught as a collection of phrases that work. The phrases change with every model release; what does not change is that you are specifying a task to a system that has no background knowledge of your situation, cannot ask a colleague, and will interpret every ambiguity in whichever way its training distribution favours.

A specification that works contains six things, and when a result disappoints the missing element is almost always one of them.

ElementWeakStrong
Objective“Compare the suppliers”“Produce a four-page memo recommending one supplier, for a decision meeting on Thursday”
Background(omitted)“We run these units continuously; sustained throughput matters more than peak”
Constraints“Be accurate”“Never present a vendor's stated figure as a measurement. Cite a page number for every claim”
Required output“Write it up”“Comparison table, then conflicts, then assumptions, then recommendation”
Acceptance criteria(omitted)“Every numeric claim traceable to a page or a row range”
Stated uncertainty(omitted)“List what you could not verify and what you assumed”

The last two are the ones most often omitted and the ones that change the result most. Acceptance criteria convert a vague request into something checkable, which matters for you and matters more in Module 11 when the work is delegated. The request for stated uncertainty converts a confident answer into a reviewable one; on the supplier assessment it is what produces the admission that a figure came from a summary table rather than from the measurements, which is precisely the error you were most at risk of accepting.

Two techniques genuinely survive model changes. One concrete example of the output you want is worth more than several sentences describing it. And naming the specific source to rely on — this appendix, this column, this previous memo — does more than any adjective, because it changes what is in the context rather than merely requesting better behaviour.

What has stopped mattering, if it ever did: elaborate role-play preambles, politeness, offers of reward, threats, and instructing a reasoning model to think step by step when it already does and you are paying for it twice.

Module 10

Harnesses, interaction modes and autonomy

Almost everything that distinguishes one AI product from another is the software around the model rather than the model itself. This module names those layers, then uses them to decide how much independence to grant on a given task.

10.1

The layers of an AI system

“The AI” is not one thing, and keeping its parts distinct resolves a surprising number of arguments about what these systems can and cannot do.

LayerWhat it isWho controls it
ModelArchitecture plus trained parametersThe provider; fixed once trained
InferenceRunning the forward pass and sampling a tokenThe provider; you set temperature and effort where exposed
System instructionsText the vendor places before everything elseThe vendor, usually invisible to you
Your instructionsPersistent preferences and project conventionsYou — Module 12
Context managementChoosing, ordering and truncating what the model seesThe harness, with your influence over inputs
ToolsCode the system may execute on the model's requestThe harness and your permissions — Module 13
The loopDeciding whether to call again, and when to stopThe harness and your choice of mode
InterfaceWhat you see and approveThe product

The rows in the middle are where your leverage is, and they are also where products differ most. Two tools using the identical model behave differently because they assemble context differently, offer different tools, and stop at different points. This is why “which model is best” is usually the wrong question to ask about a product, and why a capability you saw demonstrated in one tool may be absent in another running the same model underneath.

It also settles a recurring confusion about what an instruction can accomplish. Telling a model not to do something shifts a probability. Removing the tool that does it removes the possibility. The first is a request to a probabilistic process; the second is a property of the system. Module 14.4 returns to this, and it is the single most important distinction in this module.

10.2

Four ways of working

Products give these different names, and the names change. What follows are the four underlying patterns, which do not.

Conversational. You ask, the system answers, nothing happens outside the exchange. The model may read material you provide, but it does not act. Latency is low, cost is low, and you adjudicate every step. This is the right mode for understanding something, for drafting, and for deciding what to do next — and it is underused by people who have learned that agents exist.

Planning. The system analyses the task and produces a proposed approach without carrying it out. You read the plan, correct it, and only then authorise execution. The value is that a wrong assumption surfaces in a cheap artefact rather than in expensive work, and Module 11 is largely about this.

Interactive execution. The system carries out steps, using tools, pausing at meaningful points for approval or clarification. You see each action before or shortly after it happens. This is the default for most substantial work, because it combines real capability with real supervision.

Autonomous execution. The system works through many steps with few or no interruptions, within whatever permissions it was granted, and presents a result. Throughput is high and your attention cost per task is low, but errors compound unobserved and you review an outcome rather than a process.

In the supplier assessment all four have a natural place. Conversational mode works for asking what each report says about a particular metric. Planning mode produces the decomposition that Module 11 develops. Interactive execution suits the extraction work, where you want to see that the figures pulled from page 34 are the right figures. Autonomous execution suits the repeat run next quarter, once the process is established and the checks are in place.

10.3

Choosing the mode

Six considerations decide it, and they can be worked through in under a minute once they are familiar.

How ambiguous is the task? An ambiguous task given to an autonomous agent is an invitation for it to resolve the ambiguity itself, confidently and invisibly. Ambiguity argues for conversation or planning first.

How reversible are the actions? Drafting a memo is reversible; sending it to the supplier is not. Irreversibility argues for approval at the step that matters, which is often the only step that matters.

What does a mistake cost? A wrong figure in an internal note is embarrassing; a wrong figure in a procurement decision is expensive. This is the dominant term and is frequently not considered at all.

Can success be checked automatically? If there is a check the system can run itself, autonomy is far safer, because the loop can catch its own errors — the point Module 5.9 made about feedback. If the only check is a person reading carefully, autonomy merely defers the work.

What can the tools reach? An agent that can only read is a different proposition from one that can write to a shared location or send messages. Permissions bound the damage regardless of what the model is persuaded to do.

How much judgement does it need? Work requiring knowledge of your organisation that is nowhere written down cannot be delegated to a system that does not have it, however capable.

Two defaults follow from these, and they are worth stating plainly because they run against the prevailing enthusiasm. Start in the least autonomous mode that could work and escalate when the task proves routine; it is far easier to grant autonomy to a process you understand than to withdraw it from one you have already come to rely on. And prefer interactive execution for anything done for the first time, because the purpose of the first run is to find out where the process goes wrong, which is information you only get by watching it.

Exercise 10.1

For each, choose a mode and give the deciding consideration: (a) summarising one vendor report you will read anyway; (b) producing the quarterly comparison for the fourth time, with checks that have held each time; (c) drafting the email that tells two vendors they were unsuccessful.

Expected answer

(a) Conversational. The task is bounded, you are the check, and nothing acts — adding autonomy buys nothing. (b) Autonomous is now reasonable: the task is unambiguous, has been through three supervised runs, and has established checks, which is exactly the progression the defaults recommend. (c) Interactive at most, with approval before sending. The deciding consideration is irreversibility rather than difficulty; the drafting is easy and the sending cannot be undone, so the approval belongs at the send step rather than at the drafting step.

10.4

What autonomy costs

Autonomy is usually discussed as a capability. It is more usefully understood as a transfer: you give up observation in exchange for throughput, and several specific things follow from the mechanism rather than from pessimism.

Errors compound. Module 5.9 made the arithmetic point — fifteen steps at ninety-five per cent reliability each is a coin flip overall — and the compounding is invisible while it happens because each individual step looked reasonable.

Wrong directions are pursued thoroughly. A system that has settled on a mistaken interpretation does not become hesitant; it continues competently, and the quantity of work produced before anyone notices scales with the autonomy granted.

Context degrades as the run proceeds, exactly as section 9.4 describes, so the later steps of a long autonomous run are taken with the original instructions diluted or summarised away. Long runs are therefore least reliable at the point where they are producing the final output.

And untrusted material becomes considerably more dangerous, because an autonomous system acts on what it reads without anyone seeing it first. The supplier's hidden instruction from Module 6.2 is caught immediately in interactive mode, when you watch the assistant produce a summary that mentions no latency figures and ask why. In autonomous mode it arrives as a finished memo.

None of this argues against autonomy. It argues for earning it: run the process supervised until you know where it fails, build a check for that failure, and then let it run. That sequence is the subject of Modules 13 and 14.

Module 11

Planning, decomposition and orchestration

Large tasks fail for structural reasons, not because the model is not clever enough. This module is about splitting work so that each piece fits what a model does well, and about the overheads that splitting introduces — which are real and routinely ignored.

11.1

Why planning is a separate step

Hand the supplier assessment to a capable model in one instruction and the result is usually disappointing in a characteristic way. It is well organised, it reads fluently, and it is thin: a comparison of what the three vendors claim, with little reconciliation against the measurements, no explicit treatment of the differing definitions, and assumptions left implicit. Nothing went wrong mechanically. The model produced a plausible continuation of a request that did not specify any of those things.

Three mechanisms from Part One combine to produce this. The context cannot hold everything at once, so material competes. A single forward pass has fixed depth, so a task requiring many inferential steps either gets them as generated tokens or does not get them at all. And an under-specified goal has no stopping condition, so the system stops when the output looks complete rather than when it is complete.

Planning addresses all three at once, and cheaply. Asking for a plan rather than a result produces a short artefact — perhaps fifteen hundred tokens — in which the task is broken into pieces, the missing information is named, and the criteria are made explicit. You read it in two minutes. If the model has misunderstood what throughput means in your context, you find out there, before a hundred thousand tokens have been processed on a misunderstanding.

The economics are strongly in favour. Using the illustrative rates from Module 8.6, a planning pass costs around three cents and a full attempt costs around thirty-eight. Catching a misunderstanding in the plan rather than in the output saves an entire run, and more importantly saves the twenty minutes of your time that reviewing a wrong memo would consume.

Planning is not free of failure modes. A plan is generated text and subject to everything in Module 4: it can be confidently wrong, it can invent a step that is not possible, and it can omit something it never considered. Its value is that these failures are visible in an artefact you can read in two minutes, rather than embedded in work you have to reverse.

11.2

The plan as an artefact

A plan worth having is more than a list of steps. What makes it useful is that it can be checked before anything is executed and handed over afterwards, which means it has to carry the things a reader needs in order to disagree with it.

Ask for eight elements. The objective, restated in the model's own words, which is where misunderstandings become visible. The missing information — what the model would need and does not have, which on the supplier assessment reliably surfaces questions nobody had asked, such as whether the measurements were taken under the conditions the vendors specify. The deliverable, described concretely. The constraints and acceptance criteria. The decomposition into subtasks, each stated as something a competent person could do without further explanation. The dependencies between them. A recommended model and mode per subtask, with reasons. And the integration step, which is where the pieces come back together and which is where decomposed work most often fails.

For the supplier assessment a reasonable plan decomposes roughly as follows, and the shape generalises to most document-and-data work:

Subtask Depends on Suggested Mode 1 Extract performance claims from — small model interactive each report, with page numbers × 3 reports 2 Normalise the three definitions 1 large model conversational of "throughput" into one table 3 Compute measured medians per — tool, not a autonomous vendor from the spreadsheet model 4 Compare claimed against measured 2, 3 large model interactive and list every conflict 5 Draft the memo to the template 4 large model interactive 6 Verify every figure against its 5 human + tool — cited source

Two features of that table are worth noticing. Subtask 3 is not given to a model at all: computing a median over twelve thousand rows is arithmetic, and Module 4.8 explains why asking a language model to do it produces a plausible number rather than a correct one. Part of the value of planning is identifying which pieces should not involve a model. And subtask 2 is the one requiring genuine judgement — three vendors measuring different things and calling them the same word — which is why it gets the capable model while the extraction does not.

Exercise 11.1

Take a recurring task of your own and ask a capable model for a plan in the eight-part form above, with an explicit instruction not to begin the work. Read the “missing information” section first. How many of the questions it raises had you already answered in your own head without writing down?

Expected answer

Typically most of them, and that is the point of the exercise. Knowledge you hold implicitly is invisible to a system that has only your prompt, and the planning step is the cheapest available method for discovering what you failed to say. A common and useful outcome is that the list of missing information becomes the first half of a better prompt, which you then reuse.

11.3

Delegating to smaller models

Once a task is decomposed, the pieces are not equally demanding, and the observation that follows is the basis of most cost-effective AI workflows: use the capable model where judgement is required and a cheaper one where the work is mechanical.

Mechanical, here, has a reasonably precise meaning. A subtask is mechanical when success is defined by a rule rather than by taste — extracting every figure described as throughput along with its page number is mechanical, because whether a figure was extracted correctly has an answer. Deciding whether vendor A's sustained throughput is comparable to vendor B's peak throughput is not mechanical, because it requires knowing what your organisation cares about.

Module 8.6 quantified the result for the supplier assessment: planning and synthesis on the capable model, extraction and data summarisation on the cheap one, at about a third of the single-model cost. The quality is often better rather than merely cheaper, for a reason worth understanding. Each subtask runs with a context containing only its own material, so the extraction step working on report B is not competing with a hundred thousand tokens of reports A and C. Section 9.2 predicts this directly.

The pattern generalises beyond cost. A subtask with a narrow context and a single clear objective is the condition under which these systems work best, and decomposition is mostly a way of manufacturing that condition.

11.4

What delegation costs

Decomposition is routinely presented as free. It is not, and the overheads it introduces are the reason many orchestrated workflows perform worse than the single-model version they replaced.

Context does not transfer. Each subtask runs in its own context and knows only what its specification contains. Knowledge the planning model held — that one vendor's report is a translation and its terminology is unreliable — is lost unless written into the handover. This is the dominant failure mode, and it is invisible: the subtask produces a confident result that is wrong in a way its specification did not prevent.

Specification is work. Writing a subtask description precise enough for a model with no background is real effort. For a small task it exceeds the effort of just doing it.

Errors propagate and amplify. If extraction misreads a figure, every downstream step treats it as given, and the final memo presents it confidently. In a single-model run the same error might be caught when the model encounters contradicting material later; in a decomposed run, nothing revisits it.

Integration is a real step. Combining four subtask outputs into a coherent memo is itself a judgement task, and it is routinely treated as clerical. Most of the quality difference between a good orchestrated workflow and a bad one lives here.

Coordination has latency. Sequential subtasks mean sequential waits. Where dependencies allow, running subtasks in parallel recovers most of this — the three extractions in the plan above have no dependencies on each other and can run at once — but anything depending on an earlier result must wait for it.

So the honest guidance is a threshold rather than a principle. Decompose when the task genuinely exceeds what one context can hold well, when subtasks differ enough in difficulty that model choice matters, when parts can run in parallel, or when the work recurs often enough to justify specifying it once. Keep it in one model when the task fits comfortably, when the pieces are tightly coupled, or when you would spend longer writing specifications than doing the work. A single capable model with a well-curated context beats a badly orchestrated pipeline more often than enthusiasm for orchestration suggests.

11.5

Manual and automatic orchestration

The workflow above can be run two ways, and the choice is a real one.

Running it manually means you hold the plan, start each subtask yourself, read each result, and carry what matters into the next. It is slower and it keeps every handover under your eye, which is exactly where the quality is lost. For a task done a few times a year, or the first few times for any task, this is usually right.

Running it automatically means a system dispatches the subtasks, often to subagents with their own contexts and tool permissions, and integrates the results. It is faster, it scales, and it moves every handover out of sight. The failure modes do not change; your ability to notice them does.

What makes automatic orchestration work is the same thing that makes any autonomy work: checkable intermediate artefacts. If each subtask produces something with a defined shape — a table with required columns, a list of figures each carrying a page reference — then a malformed or empty result is detectable without reading it carefully, and the orchestration can stop rather than feeding rubbish downstream. A workflow whose intermediate outputs are free prose has no such check, and errors reach the final document.

The progression that works in practice is therefore the one Module 10.3 recommended for autonomy generally. Run it manually until the shape is stable and you know which step fails. Define the intermediate artefacts. Then automate, with the checks in place.

Exercise 11.2

Run the supplier assessment — or your own equivalent — twice: once as a single request to a capable model with everything attached, once decomposed per the plan above. Compare four things: the quality against your acceptance criteria, the total cost, the elapsed time, and how long you spent verifying each. Which difference was largest?

Expected answer

Usually the verification time, and usually in favour of the decomposed run — not because its output is better but because the intermediate artefacts make checking cheaper: you can confirm the extracted figures against their page references without re-reading the reports. Cost typically favours decomposition by roughly the factor in Module 8.6. Elapsed time often favours the single model, since orchestration adds round trips. If quality favoured the single model, look at the handover specifications — that is where the lost context usually is.

Module 12

Personalisation and persistent instructions

Everything so far has treated each request as independent. Most of what you want is not: the same writing conventions, the same report structure, the same terminology, every time. This module is about making those stick without paying for them twice.

12.1

Scopes of instruction

Instructions can be attached at several levels, and the levels differ in one respect that determines everything else: how often the text is sent, and therefore how often you pay for it and how much attention it competes for.

ScopeApplies toCost paidGood for
One-off instructionThis message onlyOnceAnything unusual about this particular request
Personal preferencesEverything you doEvery request, foreverWriting style, language, level of explanation
Project instructionsWork in one folder or projectEvery request in that projectTerminology, conventions, where things live
Path-scoped instructionsOnly when matching files are involvedOnly on matching workRules that apply to one kind of material
Saved promptWhen you invoke itOnly when usedA task you repeat with the same shape
SkillWhen the system judges it relevantDescription always; body on demandA procedure with steps and standards — Module 13

The guiding principle is to push each instruction to the narrowest scope that still catches every case it needs to. A rule about how to format procurement memos does not belong in your personal preferences, where it is sent with every request including the ones about something else entirely. It belongs with the procurement work.

One property of these layers is easy to misread and causes real confusion. They are concatenated into one context rather than resolved by a precedence engine. If your personal preferences say to write concisely and a project instruction says to be exhaustive, the model receives both sentences and does something between them, with the outcome determined by sampling rather than by a documented rule. There is no override semantics to rely on. Remove the contradiction instead.

In the organisation's environment these scopes map onto specific files and settings whose names change: personal instructions in the editor's settings, project instructions in a file at the project root, path-scoped instructions in a dedicated folder with a pattern declaring what they match, and saved prompts invoked by name. Check the current documentation for exact locations — the concepts above are stable and the file names are not. Verified October 2026 against secondary sources; primary documentation was not reachable from this environment.

12.2

Building a style profile

The highest-value personalisation for most knowledge workers is a short description of how you want things written. It is also the one people write worst, because the natural impulse is to describe good writing in general rather than to specify the decisions you actually want made differently.

“Write clearly and professionally” changes nothing: the model's prior already weights clear professional prose heavily, and the sentence eliminates no possibility. A useful profile names choices. Established style guides are a good source for these, because they have already done the work of turning preferences into decisions — the Google and Microsoft developer writing guides both favour the active voice, second person, present tense, sentence-case headings and short sentences, and each of those is a specific, checkable constraint rather than an aspiration.

A profile that works is shorter than people expect and reads like this:

Write in British English, active voice, present tense. Address the reader as "you". Sentence case for all headings. Prefer one idea per sentence; split anything over about 25 words. Do not open with a summary of what you are about to say. No bullet list where two sentences would do. Numbers: digits for 10 and above, words below, except in tables. When uncertain, say so in the sentence rather than in a caveat at the end. Never use "leverage", "utilise", "robust" or "seamless".

Eight lines, every one of which rules something out. The last is doing more work than it appears: a short list of banned words is the most reliable way to remove a register you dislike, because a word is a checkable thing in a way that a tone is not.

Two cautions. A style profile shapes expression, not substance, and a well-written wrong answer is still wrong. And profiles accumulate: it is worth re-reading yours every few months and deleting whatever you added for one task and never needed again, because every line is being sent with every request you make.

Exercise 12.1

Take something you wrote recently and something a model wrote for you. List five specific, checkable differences — not impressions. Turn each into a line of a style profile, then test it by asking for the same piece again.

Expected answer

The useful outcome is discovering how much of your reaction to generated prose comes down to a handful of mechanical habits: sentence length, how often a paragraph opens by restating the question, the frequency of lists, a particular vocabulary. Four or five specific rules usually capture most of the gap. If you cannot make a difference specific and checkable, it probably belongs in a worked example rather than in a rule — showing one paragraph in the register you want outperforms describing it.

12.3

Output templates and conventions

Beyond how sentences are written, most recurring work has a shape: a report that always has the same sections, a comparison that always covers the same dimensions, a summary that always ends with open questions. Specifying that shape once is worth more than specifying it every time, and it has a benefit beyond convenience — a fixed shape makes an output checkable. A memo that must contain an assumptions section visibly either does or does not.

The supplier assessment has an obvious template, and writing it down converts a vague request into a specification:

TECHNICAL COMPARISON MEMO 1 Recommendation one paragraph, the decision and its single strongest reason 2 Comparison table one row per criterion, one column per vendor, every cell either a figure with a source or "not stated" 3 Conflicts each one naming both sources and the size of the gap 4 Assumptions what we assumed, and what changes if it is wrong 5 Not assessed what this memo does not cover, explicitly 6 Sources page references for every claim above Rules A vendor's own figure is never reported as a measurement. Any cell without a source reads "not stated" — never an estimate. Section 5 is mandatory and may not be empty.

Section 5 is the one worth defending. A template that only asks what was found encourages a document that appears complete; a template that requires stating what was not assessed makes the boundary of the work visible, and that is usually the information a decision-maker most needs and least often receives.

Templates also govern design and presentation where that matters — a standard set of slide layouts, a convention for how figures are labelled, a rule that every chart states its units. The principle does not change: specify the decisions, keep the list short, and prefer one example over three sentences of description.

12.4

When persistent instructions hurt

Persistent instructions have a cost that compounds quietly, and it is worth naming the failure modes because they arrive gradually.

They are sent with every request, including every request they are irrelevant to. A thousand-token profile covering five kinds of work means that four-fifths of it is noise on any given task — occupying budget, and competing for attention with the material that matters.

They accumulate contradictions. Each line was added for a reason, those reasons expire, and nobody removes anything. Six months later the file contains two rules that cannot both be satisfied and a third describing a process that no longer exists.

They encode a moment. An instruction written when a particular model struggled with something, retained after the model improved, is now constraining behaviour for a reason that has gone.

And they can be wrong in a way that is hard to detect, because the instruction is applied invisibly. If your profile says to prefer concision and you then ask for a thorough analysis, you may receive something thinner than you wanted without any indication why.

Three habits keep them useful. Review and prune on a schedule — quarterly is enough, and deleting is the main activity. Keep narrow-scope instructions narrow rather than promoting them to personal preferences the first time they are useful elsewhere. And when something behaves unexpectedly, read your persistent instructions before blaming the model, because a forgotten line is a more common explanation than a model regression.

Module 13

Skills, agents and tools

Turning something that worked once into something that works every time. This module sorts out a vocabulary that has become genuinely confusing, then builds a reusable skill for the supplier assessment without writing any code.

13.1

Sorting out the vocabulary

Several overlapping mechanisms have arrived in quick succession and the names are used loosely. The distinctions that matter are two: who decides to load this, and does it supply text or the ability to act.

ThingWhat it suppliesWho activates it
PromptText, onceYou, by typing it
Saved prompt fileText, reused verbatimYou, by name
Persistent instructionsText, always presentNobody — always on
SkillText, plus optional files and scripts, loaded on demandThe model, when it judges it relevant
Custom agentA named configuration: instructions, tool set, sometimes a modelYou, by selecting it
SubagentThe same, invoked inside a larger workflow with its own contextThe orchestrating system
ToolThe ability to do something outside text generationThe model requests; the harness executes
MCP serverA package of tools and resources from a providerConfigured once; tools then available
HookCode that runs at a fixed point, every timeNobody — it is deterministic

The last row is the one most worth understanding, because it marks a boundary that instructions cannot cross. A hook is ordinary software triggered by an event: it runs, every time, regardless of what the model decides. An instruction saying “always check the figures before writing the memo” shifts a probability. A hook that runs a checking script before the file is written either runs or does not. Module 10.1 made this point about tools and it applies with full force here: when something must happen, implement it as a mechanism rather than requesting it in text.

13.2

What a skill is

A skill packages a procedure so that it is available when relevant and costs nothing when it is not. The mechanism is progressive disclosure, introduced in Module 5.10, and it exists because of the context budget: a dozen detailed procedures held permanently in context would consume the window on every request, including the overwhelming majority needing none of them.

A skill is therefore structured in layers. A short name and description — a line or two — sits permanently in the context, cheap enough that many skills can coexist. The body, containing the actual procedure and its standards, is read only when the model judges the skill applicable. Supporting material such as templates, reference tables or scripts is referenced by path and opened only if the work reaches it.

That structure has an immediate practical consequence that surprises people: the description is the most important part of a skill. It is the only part the model sees when deciding whether to use it. A skill whose description says “helps with documents” will never be selected for the supplier assessment; one that says “produces technical comparison memos from vendor reports and measurement data, with sourced figures and an explicit assumptions section” will be. A beautifully written body behind a vague description is simply never loaded.

Skills are generally defined as a folder containing a definition file with a name and description, plus whatever supporting files the procedure needs. An open specification has emerged and is supported across several tools, which means a skill written once may work in more than one environment — though support varies and is worth checking rather than assuming.

Verified October 2026 via secondary sources; the specification site and the editor's documentation were not reachable from this environment. Two things to confirm before relying on them: whether skills are enabled in your environment and whether any setting governs that, and whether your tool selects skills automatically or requires you to invoke them by name. Placing a file in a folder does not by itself guarantee that a given tool will discover or run it.

13.3

Building a skill

The supplier assessment recurs quarterly, which makes it worth packaging. What follows is the content of a skill; no programming is involved, because a skill is a description of a procedure written in ordinary language.

name: technical-comparison-memo description: Produces a technical comparison memo from vendor reports and measurement data. Use when comparing suppliers, products or proposals where claimed figures must be checked against measured ones. PROCEDURE 1 Confirm the inputs: which reports, which measurement file, which criteria. If the criteria are not given, ask. Do not infer them. 2 For each report, extract every figure relating to the agreed criteria, with its page number. Record "not stated" where absent — never estimate. 3 Compute the measured values from the data file using the spreadsheet tool. Never calculate these yourself. 4 Build the comparison table. Each cell is a figure with a source, or "not stated". 5 List every conflict between a claimed and a measured value, naming both sources and the size of the gap. 6 List the assumptions, and what changes if each is wrong. 7 Write section 5, "Not assessed", before writing the recommendation. 8 Draft to the template in templates/memo.md. STANDARDS A vendor's own figure is never reported as a measurement. Every numeric claim carries a page reference or a row range. If two reports define a term differently, say so rather than reconciling them silently. REFUSE TO Estimate a missing figure. Compare figures measured under conditions the reports describe differently, without flagging it.

Three features of that file are worth drawing out, because they are what separates a skill that helps from one that merely exists.

The description states when to use it, in the words someone would actually use. Step 3 routes the arithmetic to a tool explicitly, because Module 4.8 says a model asked to compute a median over twelve thousand rows produces a plausible number. And the refuse to section does work that no amount of positive instruction achieves: naming the specific wrong behaviours you have seen, which for this task are estimating missing figures and silently reconciling incompatible definitions.

You do not have to write this unaided, and the most efficient route is to have the assistant draft it. Run the task manually once, then ask it to write a skill capturing what it just did, including the mistakes you corrected. Review the result — it will be too long and too generic — and cut it to the decisions that matter. The assistant is good at producing a complete draft and poor at knowing which parts carry weight, which is exactly the division of labour this course keeps returning to.

Exercise 13.1

Take a task you have done three times. Write only the name and description — two sentences, no procedure. Then give a colleague five task descriptions, one of which is yours, and ask which should trigger your skill. If they pick wrongly, the description is at fault rather than the colleague.

Expected answer

This tests tier 1 in isolation, which is where skills usually fail. A description saying what the skill is ("a memo helper") performs badly; one saying when it applies, using the vocabulary of the situation rather than of the solution, performs well. The model is making the same judgement your colleague made, from the same two sentences and with less context.

13.4

Tools and MCP

Module 5.8 established the mechanism: the model emits a request naming a tool and its arguments, and the surrounding software decides whether to run it. The model never executes anything. For the supplier assessment this is the difference between a plausible median and a correct one.

Tools arrive from several places. Some are built into the environment — reading files, searching, running a command. Some come from the organisation. And some come from an MCP server, which is a standard way of packaging tools and resources so that any compatible tool can use them: a connector to a document store, a database, a ticketing system.

Adding one is a meaningful decision rather than an installation. The practical checks are short. Establish who publishes it and whether this is the official distribution, since name-squatted packages exist. Pin the version, because tool descriptions are text the model treats as authoritative and an auto-updating server can change what the model is told after you reviewed it. Read those descriptions yourself once — it takes five minutes and is the only way to notice a problem. Scope the credentials to the minimum that works: read-only if it only reads, one source if it needs one. And keep a note of what is connected, with what access, approved by whom.

Permissions deserve a sentence of their own because they are where the actual safety lives. A tool the model cannot reach is a tool it cannot misuse, whatever it is persuaded to attempt. Granting write access to a shared location so that an assistant can save a draft is a different proposition from granting it read access to the same location, and the difference is not a matter of trust in the model.

13.5

Custom agents and subagents

A custom agent is a named configuration — a set of instructions, a restricted tool set, sometimes a specified model — that you select for a particular kind of work. The value is not that it knows more, but that it is constrained: an agent configured for review with no ability to write files cannot quietly edit the thing it is reviewing, and that is a property of the configuration rather than a hope about its behaviour.

Three configurations cover most of the supplier assessment, and they illustrate the pattern.

AgentInstructions emphasiseToolsModel
PlannerDecompose, name missing information, set acceptance criteria. Do not execute.Read onlyCapable
ExtractorFind figures matching the criteria. Record “not stated” rather than estimating. Always cite a page.Read, spreadsheetSmall and fast

The reviewer is the one worth dwelling on. Giving the checking role its own context — one that does not contain the reasoning that produced the memo — makes it a genuine check rather than a continuation. A model asked to review its own output in the same conversation is working from a context in which it has already committed to those claims, and Module 4.6 explains why that matters: it will be consistent with them, because consistency with the context is what the mechanism does. A separate context is not a formality.

A subagent is the same idea invoked automatically inside a larger workflow rather than selected by you. Everything in Module 11.4 about handover applies: a subagent knows only what its invocation passes to it, and context that the orchestrator held implicitly is simply absent.

Module 14

Evaluation, safety and the workshop

Establishing whether the work is right, deciding how much to supervise, and handling material that someone else wrote with an interest in the outcome. The module ends with a workshop that uses everything in the course on one task.

14.1

Acceptance criteria

Module 6.7 established the difficulty: generated output arrives in finished form, carrying no signal about whether it is correct, and human reviewers calibrate on exactly the surface features that are now uninformative. The remedy is to stop relying on judgement applied to a finished document and to decide, in advance, what would make it acceptable.

Criteria worth having are checkable by someone other than their author. “The memo is thorough” is not; “every numeric claim carries a page reference or a row range” is. The test is whether two people checking independently would reach the same verdict.

For a document-and-data task, several dimensions recur and most can be made concrete.

DimensionA checkable criterion
Factual accuracyEach cited figure matches the cited page or row range
CompletenessAll four agreed criteria appear for all three vendors
TraceabilityNo claim without a source; “not stated” used where absent
Numerical correctnessComputed values reproduce when the calculation is re-run
Structural validityAll six template sections present; section 5 non-empty
Honest boundariesVendor claims never presented as measurements
RepeatabilityThree runs produce the same figures, if not the same prose

The last deserves emphasis because it is the one people omit. Output is sampled, so two runs differ; what must not differ is the facts. If three runs of the same task produce three different medians, something is wrong with the process rather than with the wording, and you have learned that before the memo reached anyone.

Writing criteria before the work has a second benefit beyond checking. Criteria written afterwards tend to describe whatever was produced, which is how a team convinces itself that an output is good. Written first, they are a specification — and as Module 9.5 noted, they improve the output as well as the review, because stating them in the prompt tells the system what finished means.

14.2

A small evaluation set

Criteria judge one output. An evaluation set judges a process, and it is what lets you answer questions like whether a cheaper model is good enough, whether a new skill helped, or whether a provider's update changed anything.

Assemble between five and ten real tasks whose correct answers you know — previous quarters' assessments are ideal, because the right answer has since been established by events. Write the acceptance criteria for each. Run each task three times against whatever you are evaluating, score against the criteria rather than by impression, and record cost and elapsed time alongside.

Three runs matter for the reason Module 4.5 gives, and it is the methodological error teams make most often: a single run reports one draw from a distribution. A model that passes once and fails twice has not passed.

Keep the distinction between two things that get confused. Benchmarking a model asks how a model performs in isolation, which is what public leaderboards report and what Module 8.2 cautioned about. Evaluating a workflow asks whether your whole arrangement — model, context, tools, skill, checks — produces acceptable results on your work. The second is the one that answers a decision, and it is the only one that can tell you that a cheaper model with a better-curated context outperforms an expensive model with a careless one.

Using a model to score another model's output is reasonable for qualities that resist automation, and it inherits every limitation in this course — including a documented preference for longer and more confident answers. Treat it as a noisy instrument needing its own calibration against a few human-scored examples, rather than as a verdict.

14.3

Proportionate oversight

Not everything deserves the same scrutiny, and treating all output identically means either wasting attention on the trivial or under-checking the consequential. The useful variable is not how impressive the output looks but what happens if it is wrong.

Three questions settle it. How reversible is the consequence — a draft you will edit, or a figure entering a contract? How visible would an error be to the next person, who may assume it was checked? And can any part be verified mechanically rather than by reading?

That yields a rough scale. Exploratory work you will rework anyway needs a skim. Material informing your own thinking needs spot checks on anything you will repeat aloud. Work circulating to colleagues needs every factual claim traced to its source. Anything entering a decision, a contract or an external communication needs full verification against sources, and for numbers, independent recomputation.

The economics from Module 8.7 apply here with force. Verification is the expensive part of the task, costing perhaps fifty times the model usage, so it is worth designing the work so that verification is cheap. A memo whose every figure carries a page reference can be checked in minutes; the same memo with the same figures and no references takes an hour, because the checker must first find what to check. The template in Module 12.3 is not a formatting preference — it is what makes the expensive step affordable.

14.4

Working safely with untrusted material

Module 6 established that instructions and data reach a model through one channel, and that text placed in a document by someone else is read with the same status as your own instruction. The supplier assessment is a direct instance: three documents written by parties with a commercial interest in the result.

The practical consequences for knowledge work are more mundane than the security framing suggests, and more common. A document can carry text addressed to the machine rather than to you — in white type, in metadata, in an appendix nobody opens — and you will not see it in the rendered page. A web page an assistant fetches can do the same. So can a spreadsheet cell, an email, and a file from a shared drive whose history you do not know.

Four measures work, in descending order of reliability.

Limit what the assistant can reach. If it cannot open your internal systems, a hijacked summarisation cannot touch them. This holds regardless of what the model is persuaded to attempt, which is what makes it the strongest control available. An assistant reading vendor PDFs has no business holding credentials to anything.

Keep irreversible actions behind a person. Sending, filing, submitting and purchasing are different in kind from drafting. The approval belongs at that step, and nowhere else is it as valuable.

Make verification independent of the generating context. A separate review pass, by a differently configured agent or a person holding the sources, catches a steered summary in a way that asking the same conversation to check itself does not — for the reason Module 13.5 gave.

Neutralise the material where you can. Converting a document to plain text before an assistant reads it removes hidden layers and metadata. It does nothing about an instruction written in plain view, so it is a narrowing rather than a solution.

Instructing the model to ignore instructions found in documents is worth including and worth nothing as a control. It shifts a probability in a system that is, by construction, probabilistic. Build the first three; do not rely on the fourth.

One confidentiality point belongs here because it is where ordinary people cause incidents. Material placed in a context is transmitted to the provider. Enterprise agreements normally commit to not training on it and to limited retention, and those commitments are worth reading rather than assuming. The practical rule is short: do not paste credentials, personal data about identifiable people, or material that is confidential to a third party — and if a credential does reach a prompt, rotate it, because a transcript is a logging surface. Everything else about your own organisation's work is normally fine, and saying so explicitly matters, because a policy consisting only of prohibitions gets routed around.

Exercise 14.1

A colleague proposes an automation: every incoming supplier proposal is summarised by an assistant that can read the shared drive and append its summary to a tracking spreadsheet. Using Module 6.4, identify which of the three conditions are present and name the single change that most reduces the risk.

Expected answer

All three. Private data: the shared drive. Untrusted content: proposals from outside parties. An outward channel: the tracking spreadsheet, which people read and act on — and which an attacker can influence without ever receiving anything. The highest-value single change is narrowing the first: give the assistant access to the proposal being summarised rather than to the drive. The automation still works, and a hijacked summarisation can now only misrepresent the document it was given, which a reviewer holding that document can catch.

14.5

Integrated workshop

One task, using everything in the course. Work on something real from your own responsibilities — a comparison, an assessment, a recurring report — rather than on the supplier assessment, which exists so that the course had a worked example and not so that you would reproduce it. Allow half a day. The deliverable is not the document; it is a repeatable process that produces documents, plus honest evidence about what it costs and where it fails.

Stage 1

Specify and select

45 min · Modules 8, 9
  1. Write the acceptance criteria before anything else. Six to eight checkable statements. If you cannot write them, the task is not yet defined, and discovering that is itself a result.
  2. Shortlist two models — one capable, one cheap and fast. Use a public leaderboard only to narrow the field.
  3. Run one representative sub-piece of the task on both, three times each. Score against your criteria. Record cost and elapsed time.

Produce: criteria, a scored comparison, and a chosen model with a stated reason.

Stage 2

Plan and decompose

45 min · Module 11
  1. Ask the capable model for a plan in the eight-part form of Module 11.2, explicitly instructing it not to begin.
  2. Read the “missing information” section first and answer those questions properly — they are the ones you would otherwise have left implicit.
  3. Mark which subtasks need judgement, which are mechanical, and which should not involve a model at all.

Produce: a corrected plan with a model and mode chosen per subtask.

Stage 3

Execute with the right autonomy

60 min · Modules 10, 13
  1. Run the mechanical subtasks on the cheaper model, in interactive mode so you can see the first few results.
  2. Where a figure must be computed, route it to a tool. If the assistant writes a short script to do it, ask it to explain what the script does and check one value by hand.
  3. Run the judgement subtasks on the capable model with a curated context, not the raw material.
  4. Note every point at which you had to intervene. That list is the specification for Stage 4.

Produce: a completed draft, and a list of interventions.

Stage 4

Make it repeatable

45 min · Modules 12, 13
  1. Turn the output shape into a template, with a mandatory section naming what was not assessed.
  2. Write a skill: name, description, procedure, standards, and a refuse to section built from your intervention list.
  3. Have the assistant draft it from the session, then cut it to the decisions that matter.
  4. Test the description in isolation — does the right task trigger it?

Produce: a committed template and skill.

Stage 5

Verify and account

45 min · Modules 8.7, 14
  1. Verify the draft against your criteria, with the sources open. Record how long it took and what you found.
  2. Have a separately configured reviewer — fresh context, read-only — check every claim against its cited source, and compare its findings with your own.
  3. Total the cost: model usage, your time at a realistic rate, and the time of anyone else involved. Compare with doing it unaided.
  4. Write three sentences on where this process would fail and what would catch it.

Produce: a verification record, a cost accounting, and a stated failure mode.

The last item is the one to take seriously. A process whose failure modes you can name is one you can supervise proportionately, and that is the difference between using these systems and being used by them.

If you remember four things
  1. Context is the only lever. The parameters are fixed, the window is zero-sum, and curation beats accumulation. Nearly everything else follows.
