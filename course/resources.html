Further reading

Where to go deeper

Curated rather than comprehensive. Every entry says what it uniquely gives you that the others do not, and which module it extends — so you can go deep on one concept without committing to a course.

Difficulty: gentle solid demanding
If a link has moved, search the title — everything here is well known enough to find.

If you do only one thing

Watch 3Blue1Brown's neural-network and transformer chapters (about three hours total). It is the best explanation of this material in any medium, and it covers Modules 1, 3 and 4 with visualisations no text can match. If you have six hours, add Karpathy's Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT afterwards.

A suggested path

Four routes, depending on how far you want to go. Each builds on the one above it.

  1. Intuition (3–4 hours). 3Blue1Brown's chapters on neural networks and transformers, then play with the Transformer Explainer and a tokeniser for twenty minutes. You will understand Part One properly.
  2. Working knowledge (10–12 hours). Add Karpathy's Deep Dive into LLMs and Let's build the GPT Tokenizer, Jay Alammar's Illustrated Transformer, and Anthropic's Building effective agents. Enough to make good architectural decisions.
  3. Build one (30–40 hours). Karpathy's Neural Networks: Zero to Hero end to end, writing the code. Nothing else produces the same depth of understanding, and the final lecture builds a working GPT.
  4. Research depth (a term). Stanford CS336, the Hugging Face LLM course, and the papers section. For people who will train or fine-tune models rather than use them.

Visual intuition

Start here, whatever your background. These build the mental pictures that make everything else readable.

Neural networks & transformers

3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson) · video series, 7 chapters · ~3 h · gentle

The best mathematical exposition on the internet, and not a close contest. Chapters 1–4 build neural networks, gradient descent and backpropagation from nothing; chapters 5–7 cover transformers, attention and how an LLM stores facts. The animations make the geometry of these ideas visible — what a gradient actually is, what attention actually moves — in a way that no amount of prose achieves.

You want Modules 1, 3 and 4 to click rather than merely make sense. Watch chapter 6 on attention immediately after reading our 3.2.

LLM Visualization

Brendan Bycroft · interactive 3D walkthrough · 30–60 min · solid

A navigable 3D model of a working GPT, from input tokens to output logits. You walk through every layer, every attention head and every matrix multiply required to produce one single token. Covers nanoGPT up to GPT-3 scale.

Our figure in 3.5 showed you the shape of a forward pass. This shows you its actual size, which is the thing diagrams cannot convey.

Transformer Explainer

Georgia Tech Polo Club · interactive tool · 20 min · gentle

Runs a real GPT-2 live in your browser. Type a prefix and watch the attention weights and the next-token distribution change as you type. The temperature slider is right there, and moving it is the fastest way to internalise 4.3.

Lab 2 uses this directly. Also the single best thing to put on a screen when explaining temperature to a sceptical colleague.

The Illustrated Transformer

Jay Alammar · article · 45 min · solid

The canonical written explanation of the transformer architecture, and the piece that taught a generation of engineers what queries, keys and values are. Diagrams at every step, with the matrix shapes made explicit. His Illustrated GPT-2 and Illustrated Word2vec are the natural follow-ups for generation and embeddings.

You want 3.3 in more depth, with the actual tensor shapes rather than boxes.

Generative AI exists because of the transformer

Financial Times · scroll-driven visual explainer · 15 min · gentle

A beautifully made visual walkthrough of tokens, embeddings and attention aimed at an intelligent non-technical reader.

You need to explain this to a manager, a board, or anyone who will not watch three hours of video. The best thing to send them.

Build one yourself

The highest-return material here if you are willing to write the code. Nothing else produces the same confidence.

Neural Networks: Zero to Hero

Andrej Karpathy · video course with code · ~25 h to watch, 40 h to do · demanding

Builds neural networks from a single scalar derivative up to a working GPT, writing every line. Lecture one implements backpropagation from scratch — after it, Module 1.6 stops being a metaphor. The series ends with a transformer language model you trained yourself. Free, and better than most paid courses.

You will make technical decisions about this stuff for years, and want the kind of understanding that only comes from having implemented it. Do the exercises; the value is in the typing.

Let's build GPT: from scratch, in code, spelled out

Andrej Karpathy · video · 2 h · demanding

The transformer lecture from Zero to Hero, and it stands alone if you already know neural-network basics. Two hours from a bigram model to a working attention-based language model, with every design decision explained as it is made.

You want Module 3 in code, in one sitting, without the full course.

Let's build the GPT Tokenizer

Andrej Karpathy · video · 2 h · solid

Byte-pair encoding implemented from scratch, and a tour of everything tokenisation breaks: arithmetic, spelling, non-English languages, trailing whitespace, YAML versus JSON token efficiency. The definitive treatment of the subject.

Module 2.5 convinced you that tokenisation matters and you want to know exactly why. The most directly practically useful two hours on this page.

nanoGPT

Andrej Karpathy · code repository · demanding

A complete, readable GPT training and inference implementation in a few hundred lines. The repository to read when you want to see how little code the architecture actually is.

You learn by reading implementations rather than explanations.

Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)

Sebastian Raschka · book + code repository · demanding

The book-form counterpart to Karpathy's videos: tokeniser, attention, pre-training, fine-tuning and instruction tuning, each implemented step by step with the reasoning written out. The repository is free and usable on its own.

You prefer reading to video, or want something you can work through at your own pace with a team.

Depth on specific concepts

Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT

Andrej Karpathy · video · 3.5 h · solid

The whole pipeline for a general audience: pre-training, tokenisation, inference, supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, hallucination, reasoning models, and why models are bad at spelling and arithmetic. No code required. If Part One worked for you, this is the natural next three hours — it covers the same ground more slowly and adds the post-training detail our Module 5.1 compresses.

You want one authoritative long-form treatment of everything in Part One. Probably the best single video on this page.

How I use LLMs

Andrej Karpathy · video · 2 h · gentle

The practical companion: how someone who understands the internals actually works with these tools day to day, including where he does not trust them. Unusually honest about failure modes.

You want the practitioner's view that Part Two's Module 10 compresses into a table.

Building effective agents

Anthropic engineering · article · 30 min · solid

The clearest writing on agent design patterns from people who build them: when a simple loop beats a framework, the distinction between workflows and agents, and the recurring argument that most problems labelled “agentic” are better solved with fewer moving parts.

Extends Module 5.9. Read it before you adopt an agent framework, and again before you write your own.

Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts

Liu et al. · paper · 40 min · solid

The study behind the claim in Module 4.7. Measures retrieval accuracy as a function of where the relevant information sits in the context, and finds a pronounced U-shape — strong at the start and end, markedly worse in the middle.

Someone asks for evidence that context position matters, or you want to know how large the effect actually is.

Transformer Circuits

Anthropic interpretability team · ongoing research publication · demanding

What is actually happening inside these models, investigated empirically: induction heads, superposition, features and the mapping of interpretable concepts inside a production model. Demanding, and the most intellectually interesting material on this page.

You find “we cannot read the parameters” unsatisfying and want to know how far people have got.

Lil'Log

Lilian Weng · technical blog · demanding

Long, rigorous surveys of specific topics — agents, hallucination, reasoning, reinforcement learning from human feedback, prompt engineering. Each post is effectively a literature review with the maths intact.

You need the state of research on one topic and want citations rather than opinions.

Hamel Husain on evals

Hamel Husain · blog, article series · 1 h · solid

The practical treatment of evaluation that Module 5.13 only gestures at: how to build an eval set, where LLM-as-judge works and where it misleads, and why error analysis beats dashboards. Written from consulting experience rather than theory.

You are about to build anything on a model and want to know whether it works.

Interactive tools

Things to poke at rather than read. Two of the labs use these.

Tiktokenizer

web tool · gentle

Paste text, pick a model's tokeniser, see the token boundaries and the ids. Supports many vocabularies so you can compare how differently they split the same string.

Lab 1. Also the fastest way to settle an argument about what your instruction file costs.

OpenAI Tokenizer

web tool · gentle

Simpler alternative for the same job. Shows the token count and a coloured split.

Lab 1, if Tiktokenizer is unavailable.

Model Context Protocol

specification and documentation · solid

The protocol itself: how tools, resources and prompts are described, and how servers and clients negotiate. Worth skimming before you install an MCP server, because the security properties in Module 5.12 are much more obvious once you have seen the wire format.

You are evaluating or writing an MCP server.

Security

This section dates faster than any other. Treat each entry as a live source to follow rather than a document to read once.

Prompt injection — the running archive

Simon Willison · continuously updated blog tag · solid

Willison coined the term in 2022 and has documented essentially every development since. This tag is the definitive record: real attacks, failed defences, and consistently clear-eyed analysis of why the easy mitigations do not work. Start with The lethal trifecta, which is the framing used in Module 6.4.

You own security for anything agentic. This is the one subscription on this page you should genuinely keep up with.

OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

OWASP GenAI Security Project · reference · 1 h · solid

The standard vulnerability taxonomy for LLM systems — prompt injection, insecure output handling, supply chain, excessive agency, and the rest — with mitigations per category. The project also publishes material specifically on agentic threats.

You need a recognised framework to structure a threat model or a security review. Maps closely onto our Module 6.

MITRE ATLAS

MITRE · adversarial technique knowledge base · demanding

ATT&CK for machine-learning systems: tactics and techniques against AI, with documented real-world case studies.

Your security team already works in ATT&CK terms and wants the AI equivalent.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

NIST · framework · demanding

The governance-side counterpart: a structured approach to identifying and managing AI risk, increasingly referenced in procurement and regulatory contexts.

You need to demonstrate a governance posture to auditors, customers or a regulator rather than to engineers.

Courses and textbooks

For a term's worth of study, or to fill in the formal background.

Hugging Face LLM Course

Hugging Face · free online course · 20–30 h · solid

Transformers from a practitioner's angle: tokenisers, the model APIs, fine-tuning, datasets, and deployment. Runnable notebooks throughout. The most directly applicable course here if you intend to work with open models.

You will fine-tune, deploy or evaluate models rather than only consume an API.

Stanford CS336 — Language Modeling from Scratch

Stanford · full course, materials public · a term · demanding

Builds a language model end to end at research level: tokenisation, architecture, training at scale, systems and efficiency, alignment. Lectures and assignments are publicly available.

You want graduate-level depth and are prepared to do the assignments.

Speech and Language Processing

Jurafsky & Martin · textbook, free draft · reference · solid

The standard NLP textbook, kept current with chapters on transformers, large language models and alignment. Rigorous, readable, and genuinely useful as a reference rather than only as a course text.

You want the field's own framing of a concept, with the history and the maths.

Machine Learning Crash Course

Google · interactive course · 15 h · gentle

The fundamentals underneath Module 1 — loss, gradient descent, overfitting, generalisation, validation — with interactive exercises. Not LLM-specific, which is the point.

Module 1 went too fast and you want the proper grounding before continuing.

Generative AI for Beginners

Microsoft · 21-lesson course · 20 h · gentle

Practical, project-based lessons on building with generative models — prompting, RAG, agents, evaluation, security. Shallower than the others and broader in coverage.

You are running a study group for people who want to build something small each week.

The papers worth reading directly

Most papers are better read through someone's explanation. These four repay reading in the original.

Attention Is All You Need

Vaswani et al., 2017 · paper · 1 h · demanding

The transformer paper. Eight pages, and every model in this course is a descendant. Worth reading in the original partly for the content and partly to see how modest the framing was — it presents itself as a better machine-translation architecture. Pair it with The Annotated Transformer, which reproduces it line by line with working code.

After Module 3, to see the primary source. The Annotated Transformer makes it tractable.

Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback

Ouyang et al., 2022 · paper · 1 h · demanding

The InstructGPT paper: how RLHF turns a base model into something that answers questions. The direct source for Module 5.1, and the reason chat assistants exist as products.

You want to understand post-training properly rather than as a summary.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models

Wei et al., 2022 · paper · 45 min · solid

The paper that established that asking a model to show its working substantially improves multi-step accuracy. The origin of everything in Module 5.5, and a good example of how simple a field-changing result can be.

You want the evidence behind reasoning models.

The Bitter Lesson

Rich Sutton, 2019 · essay · 10 min · gentle

One page, written before any of this, arguing that general methods which scale with computation reliably defeat methods built on human-encoded knowledge. It explains why the transformer won on parallelisability (Module 3.1) rather than on linguistic insight, and it is the most useful ten minutes of reading on this page.

You want the strategic frame for why the field went this way. Read it on a train.

Staying current without drowning

The volume of AI content is enormous and most of it is noise. A deliberately short list.

  • Simon Willison's blog — the single best filter. High volume but consistently substantive, with an unusually good record of calling out hype.
  • GitHub Changelog — for the product surface in Part Two. Filter for Copilot and skim monthly.
  • Your vendors' engineering blogs — Anthropic, OpenAI and Google publish genuinely useful technical material alongside the marketing. The engineering posts are worth it; the announcement posts rarely are.
  • Model and API release notes — the only reliable notice that behaviour you depend on has changed. Worth an alert if you have anything in production.
A filter that works

Before spending time on any new technique, ask which mechanism from Part One it exploits. Most “prompt engineering secrets” reduce to putting better information in the context, giving the model a way to check itself, or spending more tokens on thinking. If you cannot place a claim against one of those, it is probably noise — and if you can, you usually already know whether it will help your case.
