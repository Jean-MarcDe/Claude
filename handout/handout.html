Part One · How a language model works

Learning and training

Machine learning 1.1 — rules are found by a search procedure rather than written by a person. In supervised learning you supply inputs paired with desired outputs. In self-supervised learning the targets are constructed from the data itself, which is how language models are trained: hide what comes next and predict it. No annotation is needed, so the supply of training data is effectively unlimited.

Neural network 1.2 — layers of simple units. Each unit computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a constant, and applies a non-linear function. Without that non-linearity, stacked layers collapse algebraically into a single layer, and a single layer cannot represent a problem as simple as exclusive-or.

Parameters 1.2 — the learned numbers: the weights multiplying each input and the bias added to the sum. A frontier model has on the order of 1011 of them. Distinct from hyperparameters — layer count, learning rate, batch size — which are chosen before training rather than learned.

z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b ŷ = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)One unit with two inputs. A neural network consists of very many such units, arranged in layers.

Loss 1.3, 1.9 — one number saying how wrong a prediction was, and the complete specification of the training goal: whatever reduces it is what the model learns to do. Squared error, ½(ŷ − y)², suits simple regression. Language models use cross-entropy, −log of the probability assigned to the token that actually occurred, which penalises assigning near-zero probability to something that then happens.

Gradient descent 1.4 — compute the slope of the loss with respect to every parameter, then step each one a small distance downhill. Too small a step and training crawls; too large and each step overshoots. In practice the gradient is estimated from a small random batch rather than the full dataset.

w ← w − η · ∂L/∂wη is the learning rate. Training a large language model applies this rule repeatedly to around 1011 parameters.

Backpropagation 1.6 — an efficient method for obtaining every one of those derivatives in a single backward sweep, at roughly the cost of one extra forward pass. It computes derivatives; gradient descent does the learning. The two are routinely conflated and are not the same thing.

Generalisation and overfitting 1.7 — the goal is behaviour on inputs never seen. A model with enough capacity can memorise its training data and perform poorly on anything new; a held-out set is what detects this. Large models also retain verbatim fragments of their training data, which is a licensing question independent of quality.

Inference 1.8 — running the trained model forward. Parameters are frozen, nothing is written back, and nothing you do while using a model changes it. Apparent memory is always text that surrounding software re-sent.

From text to prediction

Token 2.1 — a chunk of text drawn from a fixed vocabulary of roughly 100,000–200,000 entries, built before training by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair (byte-pair encoding). Frequency determines granularity: common words are one token, rare ones several. A leading space belongs to its token, and the model cannot see the characters inside one — which is why character-level tasks are oddly hard.

Language effects 2.5 — the vocabulary was built from a corpus dominated by English and code, so the same meaning costs more tokens in most other languages. That affects cost directly and, through the context window, how much material fits. Measure your own languages rather than assuming a ratio.

Embedding 2.3 — the first layer is a table with one row per vocabulary entry. The row selected by a token's id is its static embedding, written e(t). Training arranges the rows so that tokens used in similar ways sit near one another.

Common misreading

Individual embedding dimensions do not correspond to interpretable properties. Meaningful directions combine many coordinates, and the two-dimensional pictures everyone draws are projections that discard almost all of the structure.

Contextual representation 3.2 — written hi(l), the vector at position i after layer l, once attention has mixed in information from other positions. One static embedding per token, many contextual representations: the static embedding depends only on the token, the contextual representation on the token and its sentence.

Attention 3.2, 3.3 — each position emits a query describing what it needs; every position offers a key describing what it has; each receives a weighted blend of the others' values. The scaling by √dk keeps the softmax out of its saturated region, where gradients vanish. Cost grows with the square of sequence length, so long contexts are computationally expensive.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dk ) VAttention weights show where information was gathered from. They are not an explanation of the output, and should not be presented as one.

Transformer 3.4 — stacked blocks, each pairing multi-head attention with a feed-forward network, with residual connections and normalisation around both. Attention moves information between positions; the feed-forward network processes information at a position. Residual connections are what make depth trainable.

Logits and softmax 3.5 — the forward pass ends with one raw score per vocabulary entry. Softmax exponentiates and normalises them into a probability distribution summing to one.

Temperature 4.3, 4.4 — divides the logits before the softmax. Below 1 sharpens the distribution, above 1 flattens it. Flattening promotes the entire vocabulary, and the tail is large: with ~200,000 tokens, a modest increase to each aggregates into most of the probability mass. Top-p truncates to the smallest set of tokens summing to p before sampling, which is why in practice it accompanies temperature rather than competing with it.

p(t) = ezt/T / Σ ezt′/TTemperature adds no knowledge. It spreads the choice over more tokens, including unsuitable ones.

Autoregression 4.6 — one token per forward pass, appended to the context, then the whole thing runs again. Three consequences: cost grows with context length, early errors compound because they become input, and generated text becomes indistinguishable from supplied text.

Context window 4.7 — the maximum input in tokens, and zero-sum: instructions compete with material for the same space. A published length says what will be accepted, not how reliably material anywhere in it will be used. Retrieval of a specific detail becomes less reliable as input grows, and the middle of a long context is the weakest region.

Hallucination 4.8 — fluent output that is false. No step in the process checks a claim against the world; the model produces a plausible continuation, and plausible is not the same as true. This is the same mechanism that produces correct answers, operating where training data did not constrain it — which is why mitigations are architectural (retrieval, citation, verification) rather than corrective.

Pretraining and post-training 5.1 — pretraining on next-token prediction supplies the knowledge. Supervised fine-tuning then teaches the question-and-answer format, and preference-based post-training (RLHF and its relatives) installs tone, helpfulness and refusal behaviour — and with them a measurable tendency to agree with the person asking. Safety behaviour is trained disposition, not an enforced constraint.

The systems around the model

Harness 5.4 — the program surrounding the model: it assembles the context, supplies the tool definitions, parses the output, executes tool calls, and decides when to stop. The same model behaves differently in different harnesses; much of what people attribute to the model is set by the harness. GitHub Copilot in VS Code is such a harness.

Tool and tool calling 5.8 — the model emits a structured request naming a tool and its arguments; the harness checks permissions and runs the code, then appends the result to the context. The model never executes anything. Tool descriptions are prompt text, and tool results are untrusted input.

Agentic loop 5.9 — observe, decide, act, repeat, until a stopping condition. Reliability depends heavily on the feedback available inside the loop: when the loop can check its result, for example by running tests or recalculating, it approaches the goal; without that feedback, errors tend to carry forward.

Retrieval (RAG) 5.11 — embed documents once, find those nearest to a question, place the retrieved passages in the context. The most effective single measure against hallucination, because it replaces recall with reading. Its failures are search failures, so debug it by inspecting what was retrieved, not by rephrasing the question.

Prompt injection 6.1, 6.2 — text placed where a model will read it, phrased as an instruction. Indirect injection arrives inside a document, web page or data record from outside. It works because instructions and data share one input channel and nothing in the architecture records provenance. It is a property of the architecture; no general fix exists at present.

Reasoning effort 8.1 — a per-task setting on models that support it, not a fixed property of a model. Additional inference-time computation helps on tasks with genuine inferential depth; on retrieval or reformatting it adds cost and latency without improving the answer. How much it helps depends on the model, the task and the budget, so settle it by comparison on your own work rather than by assumption.

Benchmarks 8.2 — useful as a first filter, not as a decision. Composite indices hide a weighting someone else chose; test conditions vary between published figures; benchmark content leaks into later training data; saturated benchmarks compress real differences into noise; and none of them is your task. Use one to narrow thirty candidates to three, then stop.

Your own evaluation 8.3, 14.2 — five to ten real tasks with known-good outcomes; acceptance criteria written before the runs; each task run three times, because output is sampled and a single run measures luck; scored against the criteria with cost and elapsed time recorded alongside. An afternoon's work, and it answers the question a leaderboard cannot.

What it costs, and what the cost actually is

One instruction can trigger many model calls; an agentic workflow easily turns a single sentence into twenty. How tokens convert into credits is set by the vendor. 8.5

Token pricing 8.4 — input, cached input and output are priced separately. Output usually costs several times input, because generating it is sequential where reading is parallel. Cached input — a prefix the provider has already processed — is often around a tenth of input, which is the financial reason to keep standing instructions stable and put anything variable at the end. Reasoning tokens are billed as output and are frequently invisible in the interface.

What increases consumption. Long attached documents, re-sent on every turn of a conversation. Long conversations, because the entire transcript goes back each turn. High reasoning effort. Agentic loops, which add both steps and context at each step. Changing the early part of a prompt, which invalidates the cache. And retries caused by a task that was under-specified the first time.

The cost that matters

Model spend is usually the small term. Twenty minutes of verification against a loaded hourly rate commonly exceeds it several times over, so the cheapest route is the one that needs fewest corrections. Weigh model cost, elapsed time, retries, human verification, and the cost of an error that gets through. 8.7

Context engineering

What is in the context 9.1 — the vendor's system prompt, the tool definitions, your persistent instructions, material gathered from your working environment, retrieved passages, the conversation so far, and finally your words. Yours are a small fraction of the total. Many quality problems come from the context, often from parts of it that the interface does not show.

Long sessions. Harnesses manage growth differently: some drop the earliest turns, some summarise progressively, some retrieve selectively, and many re-insert standing instructions on every request. Which one yours does determines what quietly disappears. For work spanning sessions, end each with a short handover note and open the next with it. 9.4

When to start a new conversation
  • The task has changed.
  • Three corrections have failed — the context now holds three wrong approaches.
  • Quality has drifted over a long session.
  • You are moving from exploring to doing the actual work.
  • Before anything where a stray earlier instruction would be expensive.

Planning, decomposition and delegation

When planning earns its cost. 11.1 The task is large, ambiguous, or expensive to redo; several people must agree what “done” means; the work will be delegated to someone or something else; or a first attempt has already surprised you. A planning pass costs a fraction of a full attempt and surfaces a misunderstanding in a two-minute artefact rather than in a finished deliverable.

A plan worth having 11.2 restates the objective in the model's own words, names the information it is missing, fixes the deliverable, lists constraints and acceptance criteria, decomposes the work, identifies dependencies, suggests a model and mode for each subtask, and says how the parts will be integrated. Reading the restated objective is the cheapest error-detection available: if it is wrong there, everything after it is wrong too.

When delegation is worthwhile. 11.4 The task exceeds what one context holds well; subtasks differ markedly in difficulty, so a cheaper model suits some of them; parts can genuinely run in parallel; or the work recurs often enough to justify specifying it once.

What delegation costs. Context does not transfer between subtasks — each one starts without what the last one learned. Specification is real work. Errors propagate without being revisited. Integration is a judgement step routinely treated as clerical. And sequential dependencies add latency that parallelism does not recover. A single capable model with a well-curated context beats a poorly orchestrated pipeline more often than people expect.

The instruction hierarchy. 12.1 Providers generally train models to treat system-channel instructions as taking priority over user messages, and user messages over content inside documents. That ordering mostly holds. It is trained behaviour rather than an enforced rule, and conflicts within a level have no documented resolution at all — so remove contradictions rather than relying on one of them to win.

A style profile works when it names decisions 12.2, 12.4 — voice, tense, person, heading case, sentence length, words to avoid — rather than describing good writing in the abstract. “Write clearly” is too general to have an effect; “never open a paragraph with Furthermore” is a checkable rule. Review it quarterly and prune: every line is sent with every request, and instructions that no longer apply crowd out ones that do.

Skills and progressive disclosure. 13.2 A short name and description stay in context permanently; the body loads when the skill is judged relevant; supporting files open only if they are needed. Where a harness selects skills automatically, the description does unusual work — it is typically all the model has when deciding — so write it to say when the skill applies, not what it contains. Some environments require invoking a skill by name instead, which changes nothing about how to write the body.

Adding an MCP server is installing software

It is third-party code running with whatever credentials you give it. Establish the publisher, pin the version, read the tool descriptions once — they enter your context and are a prompt-injection surface — scope credentials to the minimum, and keep an inventory of what is installed. Enable only the tools the task needs. 13.4

Verification

Write acceptance criteria before the work. 14.1 Written afterwards, they describe whatever was produced. Stated in the prompt, they improve the output as well as the review. The test of a criterion is whether someone other than its author could apply it and reach the same verdict.

Design for cheap checking

A document in which every figure carries its source is verified in minutes. The same document without sources takes an hour, because the checker must first work out what to check. Asking for sources therefore reduces the cost of checking considerably.

Seven dimensions, and a criterion you could actually apply — §14.1
DimensionA checkable criterion
Factual accuracyEach cited figure matches the page or row range cited for it
CompletenessAll agreed criteria appear for all subjects, with gaps marked as gaps
TraceabilityNo claim without a source; “not stated” where a source is silent
Numerical correctnessComputed values reproduce when recalculated independently
Structural validityEvery required section is present and non-empty
Honest boundariesClaims taken from a source are never presented as measurements
RepeatabilityThree runs produce the same figures, if not the same prose

Proportionate oversight. 14.3 Exploratory work needs a skim. Material informing your own thinking needs spot checks on anything you will rely on. Work circulating to colleagues needs every factual claim traced. Anything entering a decision, a contract or an external communication needs full verification, with numbers recomputed independently.

Actions deserving extra caution
  • Anything irreversible: sending, filing, submitting, purchasing, deleting.
  • Anything acting on material that came from outside the organisation.
  • Anything with write access to a shared location.
  • Numbers entering a decision, a contract or an external communication.
  • Workflows combining private data, untrusted content and an outward channel — remove one of the three.
  • First runs of anything, and first runs after a model or configuration change.

What not to paste. 14.4 Live credentials, personal data about identifiable people, material confidential to a third party, and anything under legal hold. If a credential does reach a prompt, rotate it: a transcript is a logging surface. Your own organisation's ordinary working material is normally fine, which covers most of what people actually want to paste.
